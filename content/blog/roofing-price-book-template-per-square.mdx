Most roofing quotes are built by the square, so a price book that prices per square is the right starting point for a roofing price book template. The harder question is which template to use and what it needs to handle. This guide compares the common free formats, lists the line items a per-square book should carry, and shows where a spreadsheet stops being enough.

## Why roofers price by the square

A square is 100 square feet of roof surface. It is the unit most roofing estimates use, and it covers the main quantities on a job: shingles, underlayment and labor for tear-off and installation are all typically priced against it. A [free roofing estimate template from InvoiceSonic](https://invoicesonic.com/roofing-estimate-template) shows the approach: a 24-square re-roof is priced line by line, with the total landing at about $607 per square including tear-off and disposal.

Generic quantity and unit-price quoting does not map cleanly to roofing work. A roof has pitch, layers, valleys and penetrations, and a single flat quantity column hides those differences. A template built around squares keeps the labor and materials visible, which makes it easier to explain a price to a homeowner and easier to correct the next time you bid.

### What a square means for your numbers

Measure the roof in squares, then decide how you will handle waste. A [roofing quote template for Excel from Tabletemplates](https://tabletemplates.com/free/roofing-quotes-templates/) recommends adding a waste factor of 10 to 15 percent on top of measured squares for cuts, overlaps and irregular shapes. Put that factor in the estimate so the reader can see how you got the shingle count.

## What a roofing price book template per square should include

A usable per-square book covers the whole job, not only the shingles. At minimum, check that it includes these groups.

### Tear-off and disposal lines

Price tear-off per square and state the number of layers coming off. A [Staxen roofing estimate template](https://www.getstaxen.com/estimate-template/roofing) lists tear-off labor by the square and says to state the number of layers. A second layer adds labor and dump weight, so it should be its own line or a clear note.

Decking should be a separate per-sheet line. You cannot see rot until tear-off. The same Staxen template recommends carrying a per-sheet replacement price for decking found rotted at tear-off, set before the job starts. Set that number in your book now, so you are not negotiating it on the roof.

Dumpster and disposal belong in the book too, with the size, haul fee and any per-ton overage stated.

### Materials with waste and markup

Keep each material on its own line: synthetic underlayment, ice and water shield, starter, drip edge, step and valley flashing, pipe boots, ridge vent and box vents. Ice and water shield is often priced by the linear foot at eaves and valleys, while underlayment is priced by the square.

Show a markup on materials separately from labor. The Tabletemplates template applies markup to the materials section only and prices labor directly at your rate per square. That separation makes it clear what you are charging for product and what you are charging for installation.

## Comparing free templates: Excel, Word and PDF

Free templates come in three main formats, and each does a different job.

- **Excel and Google Sheets templates** handle the math. They calculate area, waste, markup, tax and deposit from the inputs you type. The Tabletemplates file is one example, and it adds a Quote Register to log each quote.
- **Word templates** are good for a single clean document. You can edit the text and layout easily, but the totals are not calculated for you unless you add them.
- **PDF templates** are fillable forms. They work for a one-off estimate you print or send, but they do not calculate or store anything.

### What the free templates handle well

Spreadsheet templates are strong on arithmetic and consistency. Once your rates are in place, every estimate uses the same unit prices, the subtotal and tax add up correctly, and you can swap a material cost in one cell. Word and PDF formats are fine when you want a clean, branded document and have few quotes to send.

### Where they fall short

Templates rarely track what happens after the quote goes out. Win rate, lead status and follow-up are not built in unless you add them yourself. The Tabletemplates Quote Register is an exception, and it is still a manual log. Word and PDF files do not track anything. Keeping your price list current across several files is also manual, so a rate change can leave older quotes using outdated numbers.

## Setting up your own per square prices

Start with your labor rate per square, then add materials with a stated markup. Work out what a complete re-roof costs you to do, including crew time, tear-off, disposal and overhead, and divide that across your squares to get a labor rate. Then price materials at cost plus the markup you choose, and check the total against jobs you have already completed.

Review per-sheet decking and per-piece items each season. Prices for OSB, shingles and flashing change, and a stale number can cost you on the next job. Put the review date on the book so it is clear when rates were last checked.

Keep warranty terms and exclusions on the estimate itself. A price is only clear when the homeowner knows what is included, such as the number of layers, whether decking is billed at actual, and what the manufacturer warranty covers. The [Summit Ridge sample estimate on Invoicefy](https://www.invoicefyapp.com/estimate-templates/roofing) lists exclusions and a 30-day validity window next to the total, which is a useful pattern to copy.

## When a template stops being enough

A template works while the volume is small and the prices change rarely. It starts to strain when you send many quotes at once, lose track of which ones are still open, or change your price list often and need every new quote to pick up the new numbers. At that point the work of copying rates between files and chasing open quotes takes real time.
