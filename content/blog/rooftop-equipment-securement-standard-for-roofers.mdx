SPRI, the association representing sheet membrane and component suppliers to the commercial roofing industry, has announced it is establishing a standard for rooftop equipment securement on low-slope roofs. The standard will set test methods and performance criteria for how equipment is attached. For now it is still in development, so the practical step is to start documenting attachment details on your low-slope jobs so you can price and specify them consistently once requirements are published.

## What to do about the SPRI rooftop equipment securement standard now

SPRI's announcement says the new standard will cover static and cyclic loading, environmental exposure, corrosion resistance and water-tightness of the assemblies that hold equipment in place, according to [Roofing Magazine's report on the announcement](https://roofingmagazine.com/spri-announces-performance-evaluation-standard-for-rooftop-equipment-securement/). That gap matters on every rooftop HVAC, solar or antenna job you bid. Until a standard exists, the way an attachment is specified depends heavily on the engineer, the manufacturer and the owner.

### Why this matters for low-slope jobs

Low-slope roofs are where equipment attachment decisions are most often made by habit. According to [Roofing Magazine's report](https://roofingmagazine.com/spri-announces-performance-evaluation-standard-for-rooftop-equipment-securement/), the standard will give designers, contractors, manufacturers and code officials consistent criteria for specification, testing and acceptance.

## What the standard is meant to cover

The stated aim is to assess structural capacity, environmental durability, corrosion resistance and weather-tightness of the assemblies used to secure roof-mounted equipment. Those are the four things a roofer should already be asking about on any attachment, whether or not a standard exists.

### Load, durability and water-tightness criteria

SPRI's white paper on rooftop equipment attachment groups the traditional methods, including self-ballasted and ballast systems and adhered attachments, and notes their limits. Ballast relies on weight and friction and has low load resistance for lightweight equipment, and ballast materials may not last as long as the roof. Adhered attachments depend on adhesive durability, which may not match the roof's service life. The [SPRI white paper on rooftop equipment attachment](https://www.spri.pdf) is worth reading in full before your next large unit.

Keep your own bids general on load values. Do not quote test numbers until the standard publishes them.

## How securement affects your quotes and scope

Before you price an attachment, collect the basics: equipment weight, mounting type and the roof membrane system. The same white paper lists what to determine on any project, including code, wind and seismic loads, the existing roof system, the roof warranty terms and membrane compatibility.

- Flag any attachment method that no published standard covers as a scope item needing owner or engineer sign-off.
- Note in the proposal that requirements are still being developed and may change.
- Check warranty terms with the manufacturer before the attachment goes on.

## Where SPRI's other standards fit

SPRI is working on several related documents. It announced plans to revise ED-1, the design standard for edge systems used with low-slope roofing, and it released a 2025 revision of RD-1, the performance standard for retrofit drains, per [Building Enclosure's roofing codes page](https://www.buildingenclosureonline.Edge and drain details often sit on the same roof as rooftop equipment, so one spec review can cover several items.
