Phone scripts for roofing call intake work best when they are short, consistent, and written for the calls you actually get: leaks, storm damage, repair requests, and the occasional commercial property manager. A good script makes sure every caller gives you the same core details, so nobody has to rely on memory after a busy day on the roof. This checklist walks through the parts of a script you need, the questions to include, how to set urgency rules, and a follow-up text you can copy.

## What a Phone Script for Roofing Call Intake Needs to Do

A roofing call intake script has one job: capture enough information to decide what happens next. A useful minimum record includes who is calling and the best callback number, the service address, whether the property is residential or commercial, whether the caller owns, manages, rents, or represents it, what they can see from a safe location, when the issue started, and whether water is entering an occupied area.

Keep the script short enough that anyone on your team can read it on any call without losing the flow. Break a call into four parts: greeting, intake questions, urgency rules, and a follow-up text. That structure is a useful starting point even if you write the wording yourself.

### The core details every call must capture

Start with the basics that let you call back: caller name, callback number, and an email address for confirmations and photo requests. Then capture the service address, including gate codes or access notes. A caller who does not own the property may be a tenant, property manager, or buyer, and that changes who can approve work, so ask the caller's role directly.

### Why a checklist beats memory

When calls come in during a storm week, people skip questions. A printed or saved checklist means the receptionist, the office manager, or the owner asks the same things in the same order. It also makes your weekly review possible, because every record has the same fields.

## Checklist: Required Intake Questions for Roof Calls

Use these as the minimum questions on every roofing call. Adjust the wording to fit your voice, but keep the information the same.

- What is the service address, and is it a home or a commercial building?
- Are you the owner, a tenant, a property manager, or someone acting for the owner?
- What is the best callback number, and is there an alternate?
- What kind of service do you need: leak, storm inspection, repair, replacement, estimate, or a question about an existing job?
- What can you see from where you are standing, without going on the roof or into the attic?
- When did you first notice the problem, and is it getting worse?
- Is water currently coming into the building, and which rooms are affected?
- Is there anything unsafe right now, such as a downed line, sparks, smoke, a sagging ceiling, or falling material?
- Is there a locked gate, pet, or access restriction we should know about?
- Do you have an insurance claim open? (Record the answer only. Do not promise coverage.)
- When is a good time for us to call or inspect?
- How did you hear about us?

### Residential versus commercial and ownership

Commercial calls often involve HOAs, property managers, or multifamily owners who need a different workflow and a different decision-maker. Mark the property type in your record so the right person follows up. Ask who approves work, because a caller who cannot sign off should not be the only contact.

### Leak, storm and damage questions

For leaks, the most useful details are timing and location: when it started, whether it is active, and which rooms are affected. For storm calls, ask what the caller can observe from the ground and whether anything was visible after the storm. Record what they describe without interpreting it. You are gathering context for the inspection, not diagnosing the problem over the phone.

## Set Urgency Rules and Safety Routing

Urgency rules tell your team which calls need a person right away. Write them down so the decision does not depend on who answers.

### When to flag a call as urgent

Flag a call when water is actively entering an occupied space, when a ceiling is sagging, or when a leak is near electrical equipment. These are the calls where a delay causes real damage, and they should go to a person as soon as possible during your business hours.

### When to send the caller to emergency services

Some situations are not a roofing call at all. Downed power lines, fire, smoke, or a structure that appears to be moving or collapsing should go to emergency services first. Intake staff should not ask callers to climb onto a roof, enter a damaged attic, touch wet electrical equipment, or inspect anything near a power line.

Your script should never give repair diagnoses over the phone, and it should never tell a caller it is safe to go up on the roof. Keep those limits written into the urgency section so a new team member does not improvise.

## Write the Greeting and Follow-Up Text

### A greeting that confirms the company and the next step

Open with your company name, a brief thank-you, and a clear statement of what happens next. For example: "Thanks for calling [Company]. I'll ask a few quick questions so we can get you on the schedule. If anything is urgent, tell me now." That sets expectations and lets urgent callers speak up early.

### A follow-up text template

After the call, send a text that confirms the callback window and the information you still need. Keep it short:

"Hi [First name], thanks for calling [Company]. We have your request for [service type] at [address]. We'll call you back by [time or window]. If the situation changes, such as water coming into a room, call us at [number].

This closes the loop, so the caller is not left wondering whether anyone received the message.

## Track Whether the Script Works

Once the script is in use, track a few simple numbers each week: completed intake forms, urgent calls flagged, and calls that reached a booked inspection. You do not need special software for this. A shared spreadsheet with one row per call is enough.

Review the list once a week. If one question keeps producing unclear answers, rewrite it. If urgent calls are being missed, tighten the urgency section. If callers keep asking the same thing, add it to the greeting.
