A small roofing company phone system setup has to do three jobs: keep one stable number customers can find, route calls to whoever can answer while crews are on roofs, and show you which marketing actually produces jobs. Most roofers start with whatever line they already had, then add complexity only when they miss a call that mattered. This post compares the common options and ends with a checklist you can finish this week.

## Start with the number: local vs toll-free

Pick a local number first. A [phone setup guide for roofers from SkipCalls](https://skipcalls.com/resources/phone-setup/roofer) makes the case directly: a local number builds trust for searches like "roof leak near me" or "hail damage in [city]," and most roof repair calls are hyper-local. Toll-free numbers only make sense if you advertise across several cities or a whole region.

Keep that main number permanent. Your Google Business Profile, website and review links all point to it, so changing it later creates listing confusion and lost calls. The same guide recommends one permanent main number plus optional tracking numbers, which is the pattern the rest of this post assumes.

### Why most roof repair calls are hyper-local

Homeowners with a leak are searching for someone who can get to their street this week. A number with the local area code signals the same thing before the caller says a word. Save toll-free for the rare case where your service area genuinely spans a region.

## Compare the four common small-company phone setups

Small roofers usually choose from four setups. Each one trades cost, setup time and features differently.

1. **Basic landline or cell.** This is the default for most solo operators. It costs little and takes no setup, but calls go to one person, and when that person is on a ladder, they go to voicemail. Voicemail alone loses leads.
2. **Google Voice style forwarding.** [OnCrew's 2026 guide](https://oncrew.ai/blog/best-phone-system-small-contractor) lists Google Voice at about $10 a month per user for business use, with a setup time of 15 to 30 minutes. You get a separate business number, basic call forwarding and voicemail transcription. It is a good starting point, but it does not give you a shared inbox or detailed call routing for a crew.
3. **Hosted VoIP business phone.** VoIP transforms calls into a digital signal sent over the internet, then converts them back to a standard phone signal so they reach any regular number ([Improcom](https://www.improcom.com/voip-for-roofing-companies/)). The same OnCrew guide says VoIP tools run about $20 to $50 a month per user, and some single-number services start near $14, with setup from 30 minutes to a few hours. You get multi-person routing, business-hours schedules, mobile apps and call recording. The tradeoff is more to configure and more to pay as you add users.
4. **Tracking numbers for storm and repair campaigns.** These are not a separate phone system so much as a layer on top of one. You assign a different number to each channel, then see which one brings calls that become jobs.

[SalesCaptain's 2025 guide](https://blog.salescaptain.com/best-phone-system-for-roofing-companies-2025/) describes the category most roofers evaluate: a system that routes calls to office staff and field crews, captures leads when nobody answers, and connects to your job management software. It is worth checking whether any system you consider can send texts from the business number and log calls against a customer record.

## Set up call routing and tracking numbers

Routing decides who hears the phone. Tracking decides what you learn from it.

### Separate storm and repair numbers

Put a storm or hail number on yard signs and door hangers, and a repairs number on your ads. The [SkipCalls guide](https://skipcalls.com/resources/phone-setup/roofer) suggests a storm number and a repairs number so you can tell which spending pays off after the next hail event. Route all of them to the same team, and check the numbers monthly. A channel that brings calls but no booked inspections is a signal to change the ad, not to add more numbers.

### After-hours and missed-call handling

Plan the after-hours path before you need it. A workable routine has three parts. Urgent leaks during rain get escalated to an on-call person. Non-urgent calls go to a voicemail script that asks for the address, whether water is coming in right now, and the date of any storm damage. Every missed call gets a same-day callback, logged in whatever system you use.

The [SkipCalls guide](https://skipcalls.com/resources/phone-setup/roofer) also recommends a ring group rather than forwarding everything to one cell phone, since that is how calls get missed during hail season. Ring the office first, then the estimator, then the owner, then voicemail. Switch to simultaneous ring during storm weeks so more people get a chance to answer.

## Match the setup to company size

Your setup should match how many people answer the phone and how many jobs come in each month.

### Solo and small crews

If you are one or two people, a business number with forwarding is usually enough. [US Tech Automations' guide to OpenPhone alternatives](https://ustechautomations.com/resources/blog/automate-openphone-alternatives-for-roofing-companies-2026) notes that a solo operator under about $500,000 a year may do better with a basic phone plan and a manual process than with a full platform. Keep the setup simple, and write down your callback routine so it survives a busy week.

### Growing companies with several crews

Once you have an office person, several estimators, or crews who need to make calls from job sites, a shared system matters more. [JobNimbus's guide](https://www.jobnimbus.com/blog/essential-tools-for-running-your-business-from-anywhere) argues that the business should run from a mobile app that holds leads, estimates, photos and customer texts together, so the field and office see the same record. If owners quote and dispatch from the truck or a roof, confirm the phone app works well on a phone and that calls and texts log against the job.

## Checklist to set up your phone system this week

- Choose one local business number and port or forward your existing line
- Add one tracking number per paid or print campaign
- Write an after-hours and missed-call routine, with a same-day callback rule
- Test every number and forwarding path from a personal phone, including the after-hours path
