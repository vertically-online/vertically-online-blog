A large roof replacement often waits on someone else's signature. A board, a majority of owners, a property manager with a spending limit, or a co-op shareholder group may have to approve the work before a single shingle comes off. Knowing which approval threshold applies, and what that approver needs to see, is one of the fastest ways to shorten the sales cycle on big commercial and multifamily jobs.

## Why Large Roofing Quotes Stall Before the Work Starts

Most delays are not caused by difficult boards. They are caused by incomplete proposals. [Excel Roofing's guide to HOA board approval](https://www.excelroofing.com/blog/how-to-get-hoa-board-approval-for-a-roofing-project) makes this point directly: board members need to understand the scope, the condition evidence, and the funding source before they can vote with confidence.

If your quote answers only "how much," the approver has to go back and ask what, why now, and how it will be paid for. Each round trip adds weeks.

## How Approval Thresholds Typically Work

Approval rules come in layers. Routine repairs often stay within the board's authority, while large replacements can trigger an owner vote. [RoofVista's condo and townhouse guide](https://roofvista.com/resources/guides/condo-townhouse-roof-replacement) lists the common pattern:

| Decision Type | Typical Threshold |
| --- | --- |
| Routine maintenance or repair | Board vote only |
| Reserve-funded replacement | Board majority, if reserves are earmarked |
| Small special assessment | 51% owner vote |
| Large special assessment | 67% owner vote |
| Material change (for example, shingle to metal) | 67-75% owner vote |

The same guide notes that the threshold often sits at a set share of the annual budget, commonly 5-10%, and that some states require 75% for very large assessments. Changing the roofing material can also be treated as an alteration that needs a supermajority, even if the dollar amount is modest.

Governing documents and state rules set the actual number for each property. Check them first. Do not assume a 67% rule applies because another community used it.

## Property Manager Spending Limits and Written Approval

If a property manager is involved, the threshold may sit in the management agreement instead of the bylaws. [RH Renovation NYC](https://rhrenovationnyc.com/do-property-management-companies-pay-up-front-for-roof-replacement) describes a repair spending limit that stops the manager from authorizing a large bill, such as a roof replacement, without the owner's direct written permission.

For you, this means the quote should go to the owner for written approval before the manager authorizes the work. A quote that sits above that limit and is sent only to the manager will usually stall. Ask early which limit applies and who signs.

## Co-op, Condo and Permit Considerations That Change the Process

Entity type changes who has authority. [RoofVista's New York co-op and condo guide](https://roofvista.com/resources/guides/coop-condo-roof-assessment-new-york) explains that co-op boards generally have broader authority over capital projects, because the corporation owns the building, while condo boards may work under stricter owner-approval rules in their declarations. Co-op boards can also use building-wide mortgage financing that condos typically cannot, which affects how the project gets funded.

Permits add another step. In Florida, almost every reroof needs a permit, and product approval paperwork often has to be attached to the application. [PermitRoadmap's Florida roof permit guide](https://permitroadmap.com/guides/roof-permits-florida) covers the 25 percent rule under F.S. 553.844 and why product approval catches many contractors and owners off guard. Build the permit and product documents into the package from the start, not after the vote.

## How to Build a Quote That Gets Approved Faster

Package the evidence, the threshold, and the funding together. A practical checklist:

1. **Start with a professional inspection and condition report.** The decision should rest on documented condition, not on your word. Photos, measurements, and a clear list of defects do most of the persuading.
2. **Show the approval path on one page.** State the dollar total, the vote type required (board majority, owner majority, or supermajority), and the funding source: reserves, a special assessment, or a loan.
3. **Break the scope into line items.** Separate tear-off, decking, underlayment, flashing, gutters, permits, and disposal. An approver can compare line items against the threshold and against other bids. A single lump sum invites questions.
4. **Compare bids on the same scope.** A lower bid that leaves out decking contingency or permit fees often returns as a change order. [Excel Roofing's HOA approval guide](https://www.excelroofing.com/blog/how-to-get-hoa-board-approval-for-a-roofing-project) recommends making sure every contractor bids the same scope, which is the fastest way to make your number credible.
5. **Give the approver a ready motion.** A short written resolution, such as "approve replacement of the north and south slopes at a stated cost, funded from reserves," lets the board vote without drafting.

## Using Your Quote Tool to Flag Large Jobs

Large jobs need a different path from a standard repair. If your quoting tool produces a firm number for a large commercial roof the same way it does for a patch, the approver may receive a figure that does not match the scope you inspected.

Set a dollar ceiling for instant quoting. Above it, the homeowner or client should receive a message that more information is needed, not a number. Keep your price list current so the figures that reach the approver match the inspected scope.
