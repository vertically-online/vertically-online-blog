Every roofing company that sends instant quotes eventually faces the same question: at what job size should a person look at the numbers before the homeowner sees a price? That dollar figure is your quote review limit. Set it too high and large jobs get misquoted. Set it too low and your team spends its day reviewing small repairs that did not need a second look. This guide shows you how to choose a roofing quote review limit using your own job history, not a guess.

## What a roofing quote review limit actually does

A review limit is the job total above which a homeowner does not receive a price. Instead, they get a message saying more information is needed, and a person on your side follows up. Its purpose is simple: very large or unusual jobs are not quoted automatically from a price list that may not capture every condition on the roof.

### Instant quotes versus quotes that need a human

An instant quote works well when the job is standard and the inputs are clear: roof size, material, number of layers, and pitch are known. Quotes that need a human are usually the ones with more unknowns. Think of the review limit as a sorting line between jobs your price book can handle on its own and jobs that need judgment.

### Why big jobs get misquoted without a ceiling

Most estimating errors are not arithmetic mistakes. They come from scope gaps: a decking repair nobody priced, access that adds labor, or a detail that was not visible from the homeowner's answers. Larger jobs have more room for these gaps, so the dollar cost of a single missed item grows with the size of the bid. A ceiling catches those jobs before a wrong number lands in a homeowner's inbox.

## Start with your own job history, not a guess

Your review limit should reflect where your jobs actually go wrong. Your own records can show where your estimates drift.

### Pull your last 12 months of completed jobs

Export or list every completed job from the past year. For each one, record the final invoice total, the original quote, and whether a change order or scope correction was needed. A spreadsheet is enough.

### Find where your margin and accuracy start to slip

Sort the list by job size and look for the band where corrections begin to cluster. You may find that jobs under a certain total rarely change, while jobs above another total often do. That break point is a strong candidate for your review limit. If your history shows no clear pattern, that is useful too: it suggests your price book is accurate across sizes, and you can start with a higher ceiling and test it.

## Set the number by job type, not one flat figure

A single dollar amount applied to every request ignores what makes a job risky.

### Repairs, partial replacements, and full replacements

Repairs and small replacements are usually more predictable, so they can often carry a higher instant ceiling. Full replacements have more variables: layers, decking condition, ventilation, and disposal. You may choose a lower ceiling for full replacements than for repairs.

### Materials and access that change the price

Some conditions should go to review regardless of the total. Steep pitch, multi-story homes, tear-off of multiple layers, and unusual access all add labor and risk that a standard price list may not reflect. Build a short list of these flags and route any request that triggers one to a person, even if the total is under your limit.

Keep the rules simple enough that your team can explain them. A short table works well:

| Job type | Instant quote ceiling | Always route to review if |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Repair | Set from your history | Structural or decking concerns are mentioned |
| Partial replacement | Set from your history | Multiple layers or unusual materials |
| Full replacement | Set lower than repairs | Steep pitch, multi-story, or complex access |

Fill in the ceilings from your own job data, not from a number you read online.

## Compare what homeowners expect when they shop quotes

Your quote is one of several a homeowner may receive. How it compares matters, especially on larger bids.

Some sources on choosing a roofer recommend comparing more than price. Homeowners are told to review the scope of work, materials, identified issues, and any exclusions in each estimate, not only the total ([Happyroofing](https://happyroofing.com/blog/how-many-roofing-quotes-should-you-get)). A guide on evaluating contractors makes the same point and adds that the cheapest bid is often not the best value ([FirstRoofGuide](https://firstroofguide.com/roofing-quotes/)).

Some guides recommend collecting several quotes. One resource recommends three to five written quotes, noting that a line-by-line breakdown of shingles, underlayment, flashing, labor and disposal makes outliers easy to spot ([ShinglesCalculator](https://shinglescalculator.com/how-to/compare-roofing-quotes/)). Another suggests most homeowners aim for three to four quotes before choosing ([A Better Roofing](https://www.abetterroofing.com/how-many-roof-replacement-quotes-should-i-get/)).

What this means for your review limit: a large quote that is wrong by a meaningful amount does damage when a homeowner lines it up against other bids. A review step on big jobs protects your credibility at the point where comparison happens.

## Test the limit for two weeks, then adjust

Do not treat your first number as permanent. Run it for two weeks and measure the result.

### What to track during the test

Track three things:

1. Quotes sent instantly, with their totals.
2. Quotes routed to review, with their totals and the reason.
3. For each group, how many later needed a revised price or a scope correction.

If the instant group shows corrections, the limit is too high or a job-type flag is missing. If the review group is large and almost none of those jobs needed changes, the limit may be too low and review is slowing you down without adding accuracy.

### When to raise or lower the ceiling

Lower the limit if corrections rise in the instant group. Raise it only if accuracy holds and your team is spending time on reviews that do not change the outcome. Make one change at a time so you can tell what caused any shift. After each adjustment, run another two-week check.
