A roofing quote that goes over your review limit is the moment an instant quote stops being a safe default. The question of what happens when a quote exceeds review limit matters because a large total can be right, wrong, or missing scope, and the number alone will not tell you which. This post compares the three common ways roofing businesses handle those jobs, and shows how a ceiling on instant quotes can keep big jobs from being misquoted without losing the lead.

## What a review limit means for a roofing quote

A review limit is the job total above which a quote is not sent automatically. Instead, the job goes to a person who checks the scope before any number reaches the homeowner. Setting one is a practical way to stop large estimates from going out before anyone has looked at them.

### Why the total alone does not tell you if a quote is right

Two quotes for the same roof can differ for reasons unrelated to fairness. One includes a tear-off and the other assumes an overlay. One carries the permit fee and the other leaves it to the homeowner. Until the lines line up, a lower total may just be a smaller job, as [HORIZON SHIELD's guide to checking roofing quote lines](https://shield.the-horizons-innovation.com/us/guides/is-my-roofing-quote-too-high/) explains.

### Where a roofing quote can go wrong

Gaps often show up in decking allowances, layer counts, underlayment and flashing, and permit language. [Roof Quote Checker](https://roofcostdata.com/roof-quote-checker) lists the items a scope should make auditable: size, material, tear-off, underlayment, flashing, ventilation, decking allowance, permits, warranty, cleanup, and payment terms. If several of those are blank on a large job, the total is not yet a number you should send.

## Three ways to handle a quote that exceeds the review limit

### Send the full quote anyway

This gives the homeowner a number immediately, which feels good in the moment. The risk is that the figure may not hold once the scope is checked, and a later revision reads as a bait-and-switch even when it was an honest correction. On large jobs, that surprise is what damages trust.

### Hold the quote and call the homeowner

A held quote with a same-day call keeps the lead warm and lets you ask about the roof directly. It works well when you have the time. The weakness is that it depends on someone being free to call, so large requests that arrive after hours or during a storm week can sit longer than you want.

### Send a "more information needed" response

This is the option a review limit makes practical. The homeowner gets an immediate email saying the job needs more detail before a price is final. The lead stays engaged, nothing inaccurate is sent, and the owner reviews the scope and follows up. You trade a fast number for an accurate one, and for large jobs that trade is usually worth it.

### What a clear scope prevents

Listing sizes, materials, tear-off, decking allowance, and permits in the quote reduces disputes when a total is high for valid reasons. A steep pitch, extra layers, or upgraded materials can all justify a larger number, but only if the homeowner can see them. [Roof Crafters](https://www.roof-crafters.com/learn/why-roofing-estimate-higher-than-insurance-claim) attributes many estimate-versus-claim gaps to differences in scope of work, which is a reason to put scope in writing up front.

## How to choose your review limit

### Start from the jobs you have already priced

Look at your last 10 to 20 completed jobs and find the total where your estimates have historically needed changes. That point is your starting ceiling. A limit set from real outcomes is more useful than a number picked from a guess.

### Check the scope lines before you set the number

Before you finalize the limit, confirm that your quote template has fields for squares, tear-off, decking allowance, and permits. A limit only protects you if the lines that drive large totals are visible. Set the limit below the point where scope differences usually appear, then adjust after a few weeks of data.
