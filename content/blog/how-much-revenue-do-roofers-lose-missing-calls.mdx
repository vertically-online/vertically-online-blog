## Why the Missed Call Number Is Hard to Pin Down

Vendor sources report very different loss figures. OnCallClerk models roughly 40 calls a week with half answered and a 12% close rate on a $9,500 replacement, which lands near $180,000 a year in typical conditions ([OnCallClerk](https://oncallclerk.com/blog/roofing-revenue-lost-missed-calls)). ServiceBusiness cites a $50,000 to $150,000 annual range built on a 22% missed rate and $8,500 to $15,000 lead values ([ServiceBusiness](https://www.servicebusiness.NextPhone's calculator puts an average contractor's loss at $260,400 a year using a 74% miss rate and $3,500 jobs ([NextPhone](https://www.getnextphone.com/blog/lost-revenue-calculator)).

Each of those figures can be internally consistent and still be far from your business. Treat any single headline number as a vendor estimate, not a benchmark.

## Run the Math on Your Own Numbers

The structure most of these sources share is simple: missed calls multiplied by the share that would have booked, multiplied by average job value. NextPhone describes it as missed calls times close rate times job value ([NextPhone](https://www.getnextphone.com/blog/lost-revenue-calculator)).

### The basic formula

- **Missed real leads per month** = inbound calls per month × share unanswered × share that are genuine job prospects
- **Jobs lost per month** = missed real leads × share that would have booked
- **Revenue lost per year** = jobs lost per month × average job value × 12

### A worked example with your inputs

Here is a hypothetical example, not a real business. Suppose you get 60 calls a month, answer half, and 1 in 10 of the unanswered calls is a real replacement prospect. That is 3 missed real leads a month. If 1 in 4 of those would have booked, you lose 0.

Swap in your own figures. Pull the missed call count from your phone system, use your closed-job average from your accounting software, and estimate the close rate from jobs won divided by leads you actually spoke to. Real inputs will give you a number you can defend, which is more useful than any industry default.

The main differences come from three assumptions.

## Comparing Ways to Stop the Leak

There are three common approaches. Each recovers a different part of the loss.

### Voicemail and callbacks

Voicemail is the default for most small roofers. The caller leaves a message, and you call back when you can. The cost is time. Most urgent callers will not wait for a return call, and some will have already booked someone else by the time you dial. Callbacks help with non-urgent leads but do little for storm-week or active-leak calls.

### Live answering services

A live answering service picks up your business line and takes messages or books appointments. This recovers calls you would otherwise miss, but it works through the phone, and what it does with each call depends on the service's script and your rules. Compare what you pay per month against the number of real leads it actually captures, not just the number of calls it answers. For a deeper look at options, see [roofing answering service vs instant lead response](/roofing-answering-service).

## What to Do This Week

Start a simple call log. For five business days, record every call you miss: the time, the source if you know it (ads, referral, Google listing, repeat customer), and whether the caller left a voicemail.

At the end of the week, count the missed calls that were real job prospects, not sales calls or spam. Multiply that by your closed-job conversion rate and your average job value. Compare that weekly number to the cost of any fix you are considering. Within a week you will have a rough, honest estimate of how much revenue do roofers lose missing calls at your company, and enough data to choose a fix.
