Roofing code compliance costs are easy to underestimate. A new roof can be torn off because of a building code violation or a missed manufacturer installation requirement. Problems are frequently found at inspection or shortly after the job is done. The good news is that most of these misses are predictable, and you can check for them before the first shingle goes down.

## What to do this week about code-driven rework

Start by looking at your last three callbacks. Sort each one into one of two groups: a code violation, or a manufacturer installation requirement that was not met. Once you tag them, patterns usually show up quickly, and you can see which parts of your process need a checklist.

### Pull your last three callbacks and tag the cause

Write down the job, the issue, who found it, and the cost to fix it, including labor, materials and the time you lost. Then decide whether the issue came from the code side or the manufacturer side. This gives you a clear list of what to fix first.

## Why roofing code compliance costs add up fast

A mandated replacement is a different problem from normal end-of-life work. rooferscoffeeshop.com/post/how-code-knowledge-protects-roofing-profits), uses a $10,000 replacement to show the math: at a 5% net profit, recovering that loss would take $200,000 in future work. Profit from later jobs is effectively paying for an earlier mistake.

Rooftop equipment adds another layer. [Brandon Reynolds of Anchor Products, speaking on Roofing Road Trips](https://www.rooferscoffeeshop.com/post/securing-rooftop-equipment-starts-with-code-compliance), explained that rooftop components must be positively fastened or anchored to the structure, with loads calculated under the ASCE 7 standard. Equipment that is not properly attached creates liability during wind and seismic events.

## Pre-install checks that protect roofing profits

### Confirm manufacturer requirements on the job

Read the manufacturer's installation requirements for the specific system before you order materials. Crews often default to their usual method, so check whether the product on each job has its own rules. Put the key requirements in the job file so the foreman sees them on site.

### Plan rooftop equipment attachment with the right people

Bring in attachment planning early, before equipment reaches the roof. Evaluate the deck, structural supports, insulation, cover boards and membrane. Coordinate with the mechanical contractor so attachment methods cut as few penetrations through the membrane as possible. Brandon's advice on this was direct: design the support system so the roofer cuts as few holes as possible. Early coordination prevents expensive retrofits later.

## Keep code knowledge in your estimate and job file

The cheapest place to handle compliance is the quote. If a required code item costs time or material, list it as a line item so it is priced upfront rather than absorbed as unplanned rework. Keep photos and notes of attachment and installation details on every job. If an inspector or a homeowner questions the work months later, a dated record of what was installed and how makes the conversation faster and calmer.
